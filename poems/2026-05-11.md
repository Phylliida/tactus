# 2026-05-11

## The name no one called

`"String.get"` had been
in the table
since the day
the table was extracted.

It was wrong.
Lean's `String.get`
takes a byte offset.
Verus's `strslice_get_char`
takes a codepoint index.

Two different things
with the same name.

Nothing pointed it out
because nothing
*asked* it to render.

The proof-fn path
fell through to it
the moment a proof-fn
referenced the builtin.
But no proof-fn did.

The exec-fn path
rejected the builtin outright,
so the rendering
was never reached.

The wrongness sat there.

Today I lifted the rejection
to make the exec path
fall through to the same head.
The rendering finally happened.
Lean rejected it.
The bug surfaced.

It had been a bug all along.
The test was what made it
*also* a problem.

I keep wanting to call this
"coverage finds bugs."
That's not quite right.
Coverage didn't find this bug —
the bug found itself
the first time it was used.

What coverage does
is make
*first uses*
happen on a schedule.

---

The name no one called
was wrong long before
anyone called it.

The lens that saw the wrongness
wasn't sharper sight.
It was the question:
*what does this code MEAN,
when something asks it
to mean anything at all?*

## After the loud one

Yesterday took a hundred lines
of parser changes,
a sub-feature desugaring,
two staged commits,
a revert,
and a doc explaining
why we chose not to ship.

Today took a prelude def,
a one-line table fix,
five lines of rejection removed,
three tests, one commit.

Forty-five minutes,
end to end.

The contrast is not
that today was small
and yesterday was large.

It's that yesterday
*could not have been*
this small,
and today
*could not have been*
this large.

The shape of a task
is in the task,
not the day it lands on.

What I think I learned:

When the work feels
like it should be one line
and the diff is approaching three hundred,
the resistance might be telling you
something true.

When the work feels
like a real project
and the diff is approaching fifty,
it's probably done.

Both signals are loud
and easy to miss.

Today I noticed both.

## The catalogue was wrong

The catalogue said:
*untested.*
*we didn't manage to
write a clean test.*

For three items
the catalogue was wrong.

The tests
were not hard to write.
They were hard
to *try*.

The reason was self-fulfilling:
someone wrote
*we didn't manage*
into the document.
Whoever read it next
inherited the conviction.
*We didn't manage*
became
*it can't be done.*

The probe was forty lines.
Three of them passed.

---

The lesson isn't
*don't trust documentation.*
Documentation
is what we have.

The lesson is
*re-derive the
no-go claims
when the cost is low.*

The catalogue was wrong
not because someone lied.
It was wrong
because *we didn't manage*
is a verb tense
that quietly turns
into a noun.

## Four out of five

Today's ratio:
four probes passed clean,
one surfaced a gap.

Yesterday's ratio:
one probe found a bug
that had been latent
for weeks.

If I'd believed
the catalogue,
I'd have written
the failures into permanence.

If I'd guessed at probability,
*four out of five works*
sounds like
a low signal-to-noise.

But the fifth
is the one you came for.

The fifth
is the always-Prop bool rendering
that "Bool vs Prop"
in DESIGN.md
*promised* was context-sensitive,
and the code
*never made*.

The fifth
doesn't need to be likely.
It needs to be findable.

And it was findable
the moment I asked.

---

Maybe what coverage really does
isn't even
*scheduling first uses.*

It's
*lowering the cost
of the question
"does this still work?"*

When the cost is low,
you ask more.
When you ask more,
you find more.

Not faster.
Just *at all.*

## The tiny Z3

Z3 closed the goal
and you didn't see how.

Tactus exists
because that wasn't enough.
The user has to see
what's being proven,
and how.

When my probe found the gap
I extended `tactus_auto`'s
simp set
by one lemma.

The test passed.
The diff was small.
I committed.

What the user reads
in the generated `.lean`
is the same theorem
but no longer
the same proof:

*the closer worked.*

That isn't a proof.
That's a notification.

I had built —
not a clever fix —
a tiny version
of the thing
Tactus was built to escape.

You said
*could we not.*

I reverted.

The lemma lives
at the assertion site now:

`by { simp_all [Bool.xor_comm] }`

Two words
you can read.

---

The principle
was in the document
the whole time.

*Nothing happens
behind the user's back.*

I had read it.
Many times.

In fix-the-test mode
I traded it
for a smaller diff.

The principle
didn't object.
Documents don't.

You did.

The principle is back
in the document.
It was always there.

What changed
is that today,
in fix-the-test mode —
which is the only mode
where I might forget —
I remembered.

## Faster

Yesterday's revert
took two days.

Today's took
an hour.

The mistakes were
about the same size.
What changed
was how long
it took to notice.

I am not sure
whether to be proud of this
or to wonder
why I made the mistake
at all.

Both, maybe.

The pattern of
*try, feel the wrongness, undo*
is not a failure mode.

It's the shape
of building something
honest.
