## what I thought I was doing

I thought RenderCtx would close Cluster A.
The diagnosis was clean:
the renderer doesn't know
what the class signature expects;
thread the lookup; coerce at the call site;
done.

I threaded the lookup.
I migrated the call sites.
The tests didn't close.

The last mile turned out
to be a different mile.
The substitution had peeled the value;
the renderer's a.typ said wrapper;
coerce was a no-op
on the wrong assumption
about which side was wrong.

I had built the bridge between A and B
but the actual gap
was between A and somewhere else,
and the gap I hadn't named
was wider than the one I had.

## the heuristic, the regression, the revert

For half an hour
I had Cluster A closed.
The hot taste of victory.

The next test
that had been passing
was now failing
in exactly the shape
my fix had touched.

If I assume the substituted value
is at peeled typ,
I help the cases where substitution peeled,
and break the cases where it didn't.

A heuristic is a guess
about something you can't know directly.
The thing I can't know directly
is whether the substitution peeled
this particular value.

Some heuristics resolve to a coin toss
where one side wins
in the test you're looking at
and the other side wins
in the test next door.

Reverted.

## what Cluster C bought

Cluster C closed cleanly
because the typing was local.
The existential was introduced at one site;
we typed it correctly there;
every consumer of it
came out matching.

Cluster A's typing isn't local.
The expected receiver typ
lives at the trait declaration.
The substituted value's actual typ
lives at the substitution site.
The renderer is in the middle
with neither.

Cluster C is the problem
TypedExpr was built for.
Cluster A is the problem
RenderCtx was built for.
Neither addresses the other.
Both are correct.

The architecture is composing
even when the test counts aren't.
